Section 01

The three text fields that Chrome Web Store search reads

Everything the store's search algorithm knows about us comes from three inputs. They are not weighted equally, and the shortest field carries the most weight — which is the opposite of how effort usually gets spent on a store listing.

Field 1 · heaviest weight
Extension name
max 75 characters, but only about 35 survive truncation in search results. Keyword-in-name outranks keyword-in-description consistently.
Field 2 · high weight
Short description
max 132 characters. Appears under our name in store search results, and doubles as the meta snippet when the listing surfaces in Google.
Field 3 · moderate weight
Detailed description
Long form, folds after two or three lines. Those visible lines do the converting; everything below the fold is keyword surface.
◈ How confident are these weightings? Google publishes no ranking guide. The only documented position is that results are ranked on a number of criteria including listing metadata. The field weightings above and the ~35-character truncation point are practitioner consensus, so treat them as directional and verify against live search results before sign-off.
Section 02

Target keyword list, grouped by competition and by which field it belongs in

No tool reports in-store search volume, so the tiering below comes from triangulating three sources: Web Store autocomplete for what people actually type, Google search volume on "[keyword] chrome extension" as a proxy, and competitor review threads for the words users reach for unprompted.

Tier 1
Head terms — must appear in the name or the short description
High intent, high competition. If a term isn't in one of the top two fields, we don't compete for it at all.

Lower volume individually, but collectively they define what we actually check.

Tier 3

How non-technical users phrase it. Almost no competitor writes this way, which is exactly why it's available to us.

Section 03 · Field 1

Naming the extension: five options, compared by what survives truncation

The real constraint is not the 75-character limit, it is what still reads after the cut in search results. In the options below, green is what a user sees and grey is indexed but never displayed.

Option A Recommended 33 chars · nothing truncated
T-Frame — Scam & Phishing Checker

Fits entirely inside the visible window, so the brand and both head terms survive. Balanced pick: the brand builds recognition for later while "scam" and "phishing" do the search work now. No wasted characters.

Option B Broader reach 35 chars · at the line
T-Frame: Scam Checker & Link Safety

Swaps "phishing" for "link safety", which reaches people who don't know the word phishing — a real share of our audience. Trades a tier-1 term for a tier-2 one. Take this only if autocomplete shows "phishing" underperforms with consumers.

Option C Search-max, brand cut off 42 chars · brand truncated
Scam Checker & Phishing Detector — T-Frame

Strongest for pure search: both head terms lead, plus "detector" as a variant. But the brand falls past the cut, so nobody browsing ever sees who made it. Wrong trade for us — a security product that hides its publisher is fighting its own trust problem.

Option D Query-match, distinctive 41 chars · tail truncated
T-Frame — Is This Link Safe? Scam Checker

Matches how people actually phrase the question, and stands out sharply among noun-phrase competitors. Risk: "scam checker" sits past the cut, and a question mark in a store name reads as gimmicky to some reviewers. Test as a variant later, not at launch.

Option E Scope-signalling 42 chars · tail truncated
T-Frame Scam Checker: Links, Texts & Sites

Communicates breadth — we check more than links. But the useful part is exactly what gets cut, leaving a trailing comma on display. Move this content to the short description, where there is room for it.

Section 04 · Field 2

Short description: three drafts within the 132-character limit

This field does double duty — the line under our name in store search, and the snippet in Google — so it has to carry keywords and still read like a sentence a human wrote. Highlighted terms are the ones being targeted.

Variant 1 · benefit-led  ·  117 / 132
Check if a link, message or website is a scam before you click. Instant phishing and fraud detection in your browser.
Leads with the action, names all three input types, closes on the two head terms. Safest choice — reads naturally, nothing feels bolted on.
Variant 2 · coverage-max  ·  117 / 132
Free scam checker for links, texts, emails and websites. Spot phishing and fraud in seconds, before you click or pay.
Opens on the exact head term and adds "free", which lifts click-through on a security product where users assume a paywall. Denser, slightly more list-like.
Variant 3 · query-match  ·  114 / 132
Is this link safe? Paste any message, link or invoice and get an instant scam and phishing verdict. Free to check.
Mirrors the phrase people type, which matters more for the Google snippet than for in-store search. Pair with name Option A so the question doesn't appear in both fields.
▲ Pick one variant, do not blend them. All three are within budget, so the temptation is to merge the best clauses from each. That is how you end up with 131 characters of keyword list. If a version needs a comma splice to fit another term, the term belongs in the detailed description instead.
Section 05 · Field 3

Google's own listing guidance asks for an overview paragraph followed by a short feature list. Only the first two or three lines display before the fold, so those lines do the conversion work while everything below the fold does the keyword work.

Lines 1–2 are conversion, not SEO

They open on the user's moment of doubt rather than on the product. Everything a browsing user reads is decided here.

Trust anchor comes next

Social Links answers "who are you" before the user has to ask. Verify the exact claim wording with legal.

Feature list carries tier-2 terms

Each bullet names a check type in the words users would search, not in internal product language.

"Who it's for" carries tier-3 terms

Natural-language phrasings fit here without stuffing, because describing scenarios is a legitimate reason to name them.

The privacy section is not optional

Users installing a scam checker will wonder where their pasted message goes. Answering it in copy removes the objection, and the wording must match the Privacy tab and the policy exactly.

Section 06

Which keyword goes in which field, and why each one is assigned there

Every target term is assigned to a specific field rather than repeated across all three. Repeating a term across fields does not compound its effect — it just reads as stuffing, which is the behaviour the store's spam policy is written to catch.

TermNameShortDetailedWhy it sits there
scam / scam checker ●●● The one term that earns a place in all three. Our primary head term, and unavoidable in natural copy.
phishing ●●● Second head term. High store volume, but lower recognition among non-technical users.
link checker / safe link —●● Kept out of the name to protect the truncation window. Fully covered in the other two fields.

Section 07

Copy rules: practices to follow, and practices that get the listing removed

Store enforcement on listing text is blunter than Google Search enforcement. Tactics that would earn a ranking penalty on a website earn a removal here, so the second group below is not a matter of taste.

7.1 Practices to follow

Do
Write in the user's words, not ours
Mine competitor review threads for unprompted phrasing. Someone writes "I got a weird text saying my package was held" — not "I encountered a smishing attempt". The first version is what they will search.
Do
Front-load every field
The name truncates near 35 characters, the short description clips in some surfaces, and the detailed description folds after two or three lines. Load-bearing words go first, in all three.
Do
Keep to one idea per line
Google asks for an overview paragraph then a short feature list. That structure is also the most scannable, so there is no tension to resolve between policy and readability.
Do
Treat the name as near-permanent
Renaming after launch goes back through review and resets accumulated recognition. The descriptions can be iterated freely, so spend the deliberation budget on the name.

7.2 Practices that risk removal

Don't
Append a keyword list to the description
No Tags: scam, phishing, fraud… block at the end. This is the most common spam-policy trigger in listing text, and enforcement is removal rather than demotion.
Don't
Repeat a term to raise density
Repetition across fields does not compound. Use the placement table in section 06: one term, one home, one or two natural mentions.
Don't
Name competitors in our copy
No "alternative to [X]" and no competitor brand names to catch their branded searches. Trademark exposure plus a policy violation, for traffic that would not convert anyway.
Don't
Promise to catch every scam
Specific to us: "blocks all scams" is both a policy exposure and a one-star magnet the first time we miss one. Keep the copy on speed and second opinions, not guarantees.

Section 09

What listing copy can influence, and what it cannot

Worth stating plainly, because this document is copy-only by design and it would be easy to read it as a complete growth plan.

Copy decides this
Which queries we are eligible to appear for
If a term is not in our text, we do not appear for it at any user count. Copy sets the entire addressable surface, and it is the cheapest lever in this document to change.
It also drives click-through from search results and install rate from the listing page — both of which feed back into ranking.
Copy cannot decide this
Where we rank among everyone else who is eligible
Active users and rating are ranking inputs, so among extensions all targeting "scam checker", position is largely settled by install base. Perfect copy on a listing with 40 users does not outrank mediocre copy on one with 400,000.
Practical reading: copy is necessary, cheap, and raises the ceiling — but the first few thousand installs still have to come from outside the store.
